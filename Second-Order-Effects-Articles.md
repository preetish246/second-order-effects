## Article 1

**Date:** January 20, 2026
**Title:** The Barrel and the Pump: What a War in Europe Did to My Fuel Bill
**Dek:** India buys less than 2% of its crude oil from Russia. That didn't stop a war three thousand kilometres away from wiping ₹13 lakh crore off Indian markets in a single session.

On February 24, 2022, Russian forces crossed into Ukraine. The first-order effect of that decision was, obviously, a war. The second-order effect showed up nine days later, on the other side of the world, as a number: $117.39. That was the price per barrel of the crude oil basket India imports, on March 3, 2022. It was the highest that price had been since 2012, and roughly 44% above the $81.5 average that had held when Indian fuel retailers last set their prices in early November 2021.

Here's the part that surprised me when I first looked into it: India doesn't actually buy much oil from Russia. At the time of the invasion, Russian crude made up less than 2% of India's import basket. India's oil import bill for the year was over $82 billion, of which roughly $1 billion came from Russia. So in a narrow, literal sense, the war shouldn't have moved India's fuel prices much at all.

It moved them anyway, because oil doesn't trade in bilateral relationships. It trades in one global market. Russia is one of the top three suppliers of oil and gas worldwide, and when a top-three supplier becomes a sanctions risk overnight, the price of every barrel on the planet moves, regardless of whose flag was on the tanker that produced it. India didn't need to buy Russian oil to pay the Russian-oil-shortage price.

### The freeze, and the bill that was always coming

The Indian government had kept petrol and diesel prices frozen at the pump since early November 2021. That freeze held through a set of state elections, even as the crude India actually pays for kept climbing. By early March 2022, government calculations suggested state-owned fuel retailers needed a hike of roughly ₹12.1 per litre just to break even on the crude they were already buying, and closer to ₹15.1 per litre once retailer margins were factored back in. The freeze wasn't making the underlying cost disappear. It was just delaying who paid for it, and when.

The Finance Ministry's own modelling is a useful way to see how far this travels: a $10-per-barrel rise in crude prices is estimated to shave up to 0.3 percentage points off India's GDP growth, add about 1.7 percentage points to inflation, and widen the current account deficit by 0.5 percentage points. The Ukraine war moved crude by roughly three or four times that $10 benchmark. None of those numbers are abstract if you're the one deciding whether to top up the tank or wait another week.

### The market noticed before the pump did

Indian equity markets reacted almost immediately, well before any of this reached a fuel pump. On the day of the invasion, the Sensex fell 2,702 points (4.7%) to close at 54,530, and the Nifty dropped 815 points (4.8%) to 16,248. That single session wiped out more than ₹13 lakh crore in market capitalisation. Over the following week, the Sensex declined 6.2% and the Nifty 6.3%, as foreign institutional investors turned net sellers (₹6,645 crore sold in a single session at one point) and the rupee weakened alongside the sell-off. Brent crude pushed past $105 a barrel in the same window. Gold hit a 17-month high as money looked for somewhere safer to sit.

> The war's first-order effect was on Ukraine. Its second-order effect was a number on a commodities screen. By the third or fourth order, it was a household deciding whether the scooter or the car goes to work this week.

### Where I was watching from

In February 2022 I was a second-year student at NTU, reading Business Analytics and Computer Science, and the war registered mostly the way it did for most people my age: as a headline, then as a market chart in a finance module. It wasn't abstract for much longer. A year later I started helping run operations and finance for my family's agricultural commodities business: procurement, exports, shipment planning, the whole chain. Fuel and freight costs stopped being a line in a textbook and became something I had to model into pricing decisions. The 2022 shock was over by the time I was the one watching the input costs. But the mechanism it taught me stuck: a war on one continent becomes a margin problem on another within weeks, and that's a lesson I've kept re-learning since.

That's the pattern this whole site is built around: an event with an obvious first casualty almost always has a second, quieter one, several steps removed, that never makes the headline but shows up in someone's monthly budget. This was simply the clearest example I'd lived through.

## Article 2

**Date:** March 3, 2026
**Title:** When Gas Becomes a Weapon: Europe's Scramble and the CNG Bill Back Home
**Dek:** Europe's dash to replace Russian pipeline gas sent Asian spot LNG prices to $84.76 per unit in a single day. Eventually, that number becomes a few extra rupees at a CNG pump in Mumbai.

Oil gets the headlines, but gas is arguably the more interesting story, because the mechanism is so much more direct. When Russia invaded Ukraine, Europe faced a real, near-term problem: a large share of its winter heating and industrial gas came through Russian pipelines, and that supply was suddenly a geopolitical liability. Europe's answer was to buy liquefied natural gas (LNG) from anywhere else it could, in as large a volume as it could, as fast as it could.

LNG is a global, ship-borne commodity, which means Europe wasn't just buying gas, it was outbidding everyone else who wanted gas, India included. The Japan-Korea Marker, the benchmark price for Asian spot LNG, hit a single-day high of $84.76 per million British thermal units on March 7, 2022. For context, that market had spent most of the previous decade trading somewhere between $5 and $15. Asian spot prices more broadly touched roughly $70 per unit at points in the crisis. Europe wasn't targeting India's gas supply. It didn't need to. It just needed to pay more than India was willing to, and the market did the rest.

### From a shipping benchmark to a kitchen stove

India's exposure to this runs deeper than car fuel. LNG feeds city gas distribution networks: the CNG that powers a large share of auto-rickshaws, taxis, and private cars in cities like Delhi and Mumbai, and the PNG piped directly into apartment kitchens. It also feeds fertiliser plants and a growing base of industrial gas users. When the wholesale price of imported gas moves, city gas distributors either absorb the loss or pass it on, and at the price swings LNG saw in 2022, absorbing it wasn't really an option.

In Mumbai, Mahanagar Gas, one of the largest city gas distributors in the country, raised CNG prices by ₹6 per kilogram and PNG prices by ₹4 per unit. Multiply that across a metro area's worth of taxis, autos, and households, and it's a real transfer of cost, even if each individual line item looks small on a single receipt.

### The quieter effect: India just bought less gas

The part of this story I found more interesting than the price hikes is what happened to volumes. Rather than keep buying LNG at record spot prices, India pulled back. LNG import volumes fell roughly 15% in the 2022-23 fiscal year compared with the year before, even as the total import bill rose about 27%, because the gas that was bought cost so much more per unit. India's spot LNG imports specifically were down around 14% year-on-year, as buyers simply avoided the spot market when they could. India and China posted some of the largest declines in LNG imports of any major economies during the crisis, for exactly this reason.

> Europe's energy security problem never touched an Indian gas field, an Indian pipeline, or an Indian company. It only had to touch a price. The price did the rest of the travelling on its own.

### Why this one felt different to me

I watched this one from Singapore rather than from an Indian kitchen, so my version of it was less about a CNG receipt and more about a spreadsheet. Fuel and logistics costs run through nearly every line of my family's agricultural export business, and 2022 was the year "energy prices" stopped being a macro talking point and started being an input I had to actually forecast around. What struck me most about researching this one properly, rather than just living adjacent to it, is how invisible the mechanism is if you're not looking for it. Nobody renegotiated a contract with India. Nobody announced a policy aimed at Indian consumers. A number on a shipping-and-trading benchmark moved because a much larger, much richer buyer entered the same market, and every other buyer in that market inherited the consequence, whether or not they were involved in the original decision at all.

## Article 3

**Date:** April 14, 2026
**Title:** The Fed Sneezes, Emerging Markets Catch Cold: Chasing the Rupee to 80
**Dek:** Six interest rate hikes in Washington in a single year, and the rupee crossed 80, then 82, to the dollar for the first time in its history. On the mechanics of how a rate decision becomes a remittance receipt.

In September 2022, the Indian rupee opened at a record low of 80.28 to the US dollar. By October, it had crossed 82, also a first, and had depreciated close to 11% against the dollar for the year. None of this happened because of anything India did. It happened because the US Federal Reserve raised interest rates six times in 2022, in its most aggressive tightening cycle in decades, trying to bring down inflation that had little to do with India at all.

### Why a rate hike in Washington moves a currency in Mumbai

The mechanism is simpler than it sounds. When the Fed raises rates, US government debt (Treasury bonds) starts paying more interest, essentially risk-free, in the world's reserve currency. That makes the dollar more attractive to any investor holding money anywhere else in the world, including investors who had previously parked money in Indian equities or bonds for a higher yield. As US rates rose, some of that money did what capital generally does: it moved toward the better, safer return. Investors sold rupee-denominated assets, converted the proceeds to dollars, and the increased demand for dollars (and reduced demand for rupees) pushed the exchange rate against India.

This is sometimes called the "risk-free rate" problem, and it's a structural feature of how emerging markets relate to US monetary policy, not a one-off. When the world's largest, most liquid economy makes its own currency and debt more attractive, it pulls capital away from smaller, less liquid markets almost by definition. The war in Ukraine layered directly on top of this: the same global uncertainty pushing the Fed to act aggressively on inflation was also pushing global investors toward safe-haven assets generally, which meant the pressure on the rupee was coming from two directions in the same year.

### What an 11% depreciation actually does

A weaker rupee is not automatically bad (a cheaper currency can help exporters), but for a country that imports a large share of its energy, as India does, currency depreciation compounds the same oil and gas price shocks covered elsewhere on this site. Crude oil is priced and settled in dollars. If the price of crude stays flat in dollar terms but the rupee weakens 11% against the dollar, India's oil import bill in rupee terms still rises by roughly that 11%, on top of whatever the dollar price of oil itself did. The two effects, a global commodity price shock and a currency shock, don't just add together, they multiply.

> A decision made in a marble building in Washington, aimed entirely at US inflation, changes what a rupee is worth on a remittance receipt eight thousand miles away, without a single Indian policymaker in the room.

### The version I actually noticed

This is the article where the mechanism stopped being theoretical for me. Since 2023 I've helped manage a portion of my family's investment portfolio, spread across equities, fixed income, and gold, and 2022's Fed cycle is a large part of why I started paying close attention to how those three assets actually move together, rather than assuming they diversify each other the way a textbook says they should. When the risk-free rate moves that hard, correlations that normally hold don't, and you find that out by watching a portfolio, not by reading about it. USD/INR became a number I checked the way some people check the weather, not because I trade currencies day to day, but because it quietly determines how far money and returns actually go when they cross between the two economies I live and work between.

## Article 4

**Date:** May 26, 2026
**Title:** Three Million Cars That Weren't Built: Notes on the Chip Shortage
**Dek:** A component that costs a few dollars idled car factories worldwide for two years running, and quietly rewired showroom waitlists, used-car prices, and dealer margins in the process.

Of everything on this site, the global chip shortage is the one where the first-order cause is the most boring-sounding and the second-order consequences are the most disproportionate. It wasn't a war or a rate decision. It was a component, a semiconductor, often worth only a few dollars, that automakers ran short of, starting in 2020 and stretching well into 2022 and beyond.

### How a boring component became the bottleneck

When the pandemic hit in 2020, carmakers, expecting a collapse in demand, cancelled their chip orders. Chip foundries, running at full capacity, reallocated that freed-up production toward consumer electronics (laptops, gaming consoles, home electronics) as pandemic demand for those categories spiked instead. When car demand recovered faster than anyone expected, automakers went back to place chip orders and found themselves at the back of a very long queue behind an industry that had already claimed the capacity.

The lead time from ordering a chip to receiving it, which ran three to four months before the pandemic, stretched to a year or longer during the crisis. Median semiconductor inventories (the buffer stock that lets an industry absorb small disruptions) fell from about 40 days in 2019 to less than five days in 2021. There was, in effect, no slack left anywhere in the system to absorb the shock.

### The scale of it

The production impact was enormous relative to how small the missing part was. Global light-vehicle production lost more than 9.5 million units in 2021 alone as a direct result of the chip shortage, with the worst single quarter, the third quarter of 2021, accounting for an estimated 3.5 million units lost on its own. Roughly 3 million more units were affected in 2022. Estimates put the potential financial impact on automakers at up to $110 billion in 2022, on top of losses already sustained the year before. It took until around mid-2023 for industry analysts to describe the shortage as "mostly over."

> The part that failed was worth a few dollars. The vehicles it stopped from being built were worth tens of thousands each. That ratio, a tiny point of failure with an enormous multiplier, is what makes supply chain second-order effects so different from a price shock. It isn't about cost. It's about a missing link breaking an entire chain.

### Where this actually landed

The consequences that reached ordinary buyers weren't subtle: new car waitlists stretched to many months, manufacturers prioritised their highest-margin trims and models over base versions (because if you can only build a fraction of your normal volume, you build the profitable fraction), and buyers who couldn't wait moved into the used car market instead, pushing used car prices up sharply in markets around the world, including, anecdotally, in Singapore's already-expensive car market, where a shortage of new inventory added another layer of pressure onto a system already constrained by COE quotas.

I didn't buy a car during this window, but I did spend a chunk of an internship building forecasting models to project resource requirements and performance outcomes for exactly this kind of question: whether a supply constraint upstream would still be biting six or twelve months out, and what that meant for decisions made much further down the chain. The chip shortage is the case study I keep coming back to when I explain that work, because it's such a clean example of the underlying problem: a small, boring input with almost no redundancy built around it. What I find most useful about it, as a case study, is how it shows that second-order effects don't require a dramatic first-order cause. A pandemic-era inventory decision, made without any geopolitical drama attached to it at all, still managed to reshape a global industry for the better part of three years.

## Article 5

**Date:** July 7, 2026
**Title:** The Long Way Round: How a Missile in the Red Sea Rerouted My Parcel
**Dek:** Roughly 14% of global maritime trade passes through the Red Sea. Since late 2023, Houthi attacks have sent much of it around Africa instead, adding two weeks and a million dollars a voyage.

Around 14% of global maritime trade, and 30% of global containerised trade, normally moves through the Red Sea and the Suez Canal, the shortest sea route between Asia and Europe. Since November 2023, Houthi forces in Yemen have launched attacks on commercial shipping transiting the area, more than 190 by October 2024, in what they've framed as a response to the conflict in Gaza. The direct target has been specific ships. The indirect target, whether intended or not, has been the economics of the entire route.

### Ships voting with their engines

Shipping lines responded the way most rational actors respond to a serious safety risk: they left. Suez Canal transits fell from around 2,068 in November 2023 to about 877 by October 2024, a decline of well over half. Hundreds of vessels across the major container carriers rerouted around the Cape of Good Hope, the southern tip of Africa, instead of the Suez shortcut. That detour adds somewhere between 10 and 15 days of transit time depending on the specific route, and roughly $1 million in additional cost per voyage once you account for extra fuel, crew time, and insurance.

For cargo actually moving to the US East Coast, shipments from the affected region were taking 47% longer than before the crisis; shipments to Europe were taking 33% longer. Insurance premiums for vessels that continued transiting the Red Sea rose sharply to compensate for the elevated risk, which pushed even more shipping lines toward the safer, longer, more expensive route around Africa.

### Singapore's ringside seat

Singapore's port sits close to the other end of this problem. As one of the world's busiest transshipment hubs, it absorbs a lot of the knock-on effect of rerouted global shipping, even though the Red Sea itself is thousands of kilometres away. The Red Sea crisis marked a real shift in container transshipment patterns, as carriers rerouted around the Cape of Good Hope rather than through the Suez, adding delays to Singapore's own imports of goods like petrochemicals, specialty chemicals, and machinery arriving from Europe. Average vessel waiting times at the Port of Singapore rose by around 20% in 2023, even as total vessel arrival tonnage set a record above 3 billion gross tonnes for the first time. The port was handling more volume and more congestion simultaneously.

> Nobody attacking a container ship in the Red Sea is trying to slow down a delivery to Tampines. But shipping is a single, interconnected network, and a disruption anywhere in it shows up as friction everywhere in it, including in ports that never see the ships involved.

### The version that reaches an inbox

This is the one I felt most directly, not as a customer waiting on a parcel but as someone coordinating the other end of the pipe. Shipment planning and export logistics are a core part of what I do for my family's agricultural commodities business, and the Red Sea crisis was the first time I watched a geopolitical event rewrite a shipping timeline I was personally responsible for: recalculating transit windows, absorbing longer lead times into commitments already made to buyers, and explaining to a supplier why a vessel that should have arrived on schedule was somewhere off the coast of South Africa instead. There's also a carbon footprint side to this that rarely makes it into the trade press: ships rerouted around the Cape of Good Hope release roughly 42% more carbon per vessel than the same voyage through Suez, simply because the route is longer. A localized security crisis, in other words, has a measurable global emissions footprint too, one more layer removed from the original headline, and one more reason the "second order" framing rarely stops at just one order.

## Article 6

**Date:** August 5, 2026
**Title:** The World's Most Expensive City, in the Most Expensive Year
**Dek:** In December 2022, Singapore tied New York as the most expensive city on earth, the same year it absorbed a war in Europe, a Fed on the warpath, and a shipping system under strain. A look at how it all landed in one place at once.

Most of what I've written on this site treats one event at a time: a war, a rate decision, a shipping disruption, traced forward to wherever it eventually lands. This one works backward instead. It starts from a single fact: that Singapore, in December 2022, tied with New York as the most expensive city in the world, the eighth time in ten years it had held that title, and asks how many of the threads covered here actually converged to produce it.

### The numbers, stacked

Singapore's inflation rate hit 6.13% in 2022, up 3.81 percentage points from the year before, and stayed elevated into 2023 at 4.83%. Within that, monthly year-on-year inflation ranged as high as 7.5% in August and September 2022, a level of price growth that a country like Singapore, with tight control over its currency and a historically low, stable inflation environment, essentially hadn't dealt with in a generation. Housing rents and car costs (driven by Certificate of Entitlement quotas that keep vehicle supply deliberately scarce) stayed firm throughout, adding structural cost pressure on top of the imported, global kind.

None of that inflation was really "Singapore's" in origin. Singapore doesn't produce much energy, doesn't set global shipping rates, and doesn't control the US Federal Reserve. It is instead almost a pure receiver of the effects described elsewhere on this site: a trade-dependent, import-dependent, currency-open economy that sits downstream of essentially every global shock at once. The war pushed up energy and food prices globally. The Fed's tightening cycle pulled capital out of the region and strengthened the US dollar against almost everything, making imports costlier in local-currency terms across Asia. And as a manufacturing and trade hub, Singapore's own export-led sector, roughly 20 to 25% of GDP, went quiet as global demand cooled, even as the cost side of its economy stayed under pressure. Higher costs and softer growth, at the same time, is about as uncomfortable a combination as an economy can be handed.

### A hub absorbs what passes through it

There's a specific irony in Singapore's position that's worth sitting with: the qualities that make it a global trade and financial hub (open capital markets, heavy trade dependence, a currency freely convertible and closely watched by global investors, a port that touches a meaningful share of the world's container traffic) are the same qualities that make it unusually exposed to shocks that originate everywhere else. Being extremely well-connected to the global economy means importing more of that economy's turbulence, not less. The Port of Singapore's own experience captures this neatly: vessel arrival tonnage hit a record above 3 billion gross tonnes in 2023, even as average waiting times rose roughly 20% the same year, because the Red Sea crisis was rerouting congestion into every major transshipment hub simultaneously, Singapore included.

> A country doesn't need to be involved in a war, a rate decision, or a shipping attack to feel all three in the same calendar year. It just needs to be well-connected enough to the rest of the world for all three to arrive at its door.

### Writing this from inside it

I spent this period as a few different people at once: an undergraduate at NTU, then an intern moving between a startup, a statutory board, and a systems integrator, and throughout all of it, someone helping run a family business where every cost the six pieces on this site describe (fuel, gas, currency, shipping) eventually landed on a spreadsheet I had to make sense of. That combination is really why I ended up back in a classroom this year for a Master's in Finance rather than moving straight into another operating role: I wanted the formal version of the pattern-matching I'd already been doing informally, under pressure, with real numbers attached. Putting these six pieces together over the past several months, the thing that stuck with me most wasn't any individual number, it was how short the distance actually is between an event that feels far away and a line item on a bill in front of you. A war doesn't need to reach a border for its consequences to reach a household. A rate decision doesn't need translation to matter to someone with no stake in US monetary policy at all. That's really the whole thesis of this site: the first-order effect is the story everyone already knows. The second-order effect is where you actually live.
